# Platform Competition in Two-Sided Markets: Evidence from the PC Game Market

*Submitted in partial fulfillment of the requirements for the M.S. in Quantitative Economics at the University of South Carolina's Darla Moore School of Business.*

Do entrenched platforms in two-sided markets face price competition when a well-funded challenger enters and subsidizes both sides? In December 2018, Epic Games launched a storefront to challenge Steam's decade-long dominance over PC game distribution, backed by roughly $2 billion in buyer-side promotional spending and a revenue cut of 12% against Steam's 30%. Because publishers set their own prices on both platforms, Steam's competitive response is directly observable in the prices and discounts of the games it sells.

Using daily price and sale data for 900 Steam games from 2013 to 2024, I measure that response at two levels. The **market level** uses an event-study regression comparing games listed on both platforms against games listed only on Steam, which is descriptive and cannot attribute changes to Epic's entry. The **game level** uses stacked and synthetic control difference-in-differences models to isolate what happens to a title's Steam price and discounting when that same title launches on Epic.

The game-level results are almost entirely null. Epic's entry exerted no downward pressure on Steam pricing in the short run, and the two significant estimates both point upward rather than down. This is consistent with two-sided market theory, which holds that steering transactions toward an entrant is weak when users concentrate their actual purchasing on one dominant platform even while holding accounts on both.
